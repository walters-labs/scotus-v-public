# User testing for SCOTUS v. Public Opinion

Use a 15-minute session to learn whether a person can find evidence and interpret the chart. Aim for five participants with a mix of technical and nontechnical backgrounds. This is a usability exercise, not a representative survey or a validation of the research findings.

No participants have been recruited and no feedback has been collected. Outreach requires the project owner's authorization. Record the tested release or commit, URL, date, device, and browser so feedback can be tied to the version someone actually used.

## Introduction to read aloud

“This application compares selected public opinion questions and Supreme Court decisions over time. We are testing the application, not you. Please say what you are looking for and what you think the page means. You can skip any task or stop at any time. We do not need to know your political beliefs. May I take anonymous notes about your experience?”

Take notes only after agreement. Do not record audio, video, names, or personal political opinions by default. Use participant IDs such as P01. Ask separately before publishing any direct quotation; anonymous task observations are sufficient for the project report.

## Tasks

Let the participant attempt each task without instruction. Record their first interpretation before offering help. Use neutral follow-ups such as “What on the page led you to that conclusion?”

1. **Find an issue.** Search for an issue you would like to explore. What do you think the chart says? Identify one case and one survey question that seem relevant, and explain why. If you prefer a supplied topic, use gun control.
2. **Inspect the public evidence.** Switch from the combined public series to an individual source. Find a question behind one observation. What did respondents actually answer? Does that question measure the whole topic or one part of it?
3. **Interpret a change.** Find two observations in different years. Describe the change you see. Can you tell whether the same data sources contributed in both years? What would you conclude about a year without an observation?
4. **Interpret the court evidence.** Find a court result included in the trend. What does a score near zero mean to you? Does the page tell you enough to distinguish a close vote from a neutral or unknown decision direction?
5. **Assess a claim.** Would this page support the claim that public opinion caused a court decision, or that it predicts the next decision? Explain what additional evidence you would want.

Finish by asking: “What was most confusing?”, “What would make you trust or use this more?”, and “What would you use this for, if anything?” Avoid asking whether the participant likes the app before these questions.

## How to evaluate the sessions

Count task outcomes and recurring misunderstandings, preserving the denominator: “three of five participants interpreted missing years as measured values,” for example. Do not generalize those proportions to all users. Keep usability findings separate from whether the cases are legally relevant or the survey estimates are statistically sound.

Flag misunderstandings with analytical consequences first: treating a search score as a probability, equating the court vote-margin score with public support, reading connected segments as observed annual data, or treating association as causation. An unaided correct explanation is stronger evidence than agreement after the moderator explains the answer.

For each issue, record the supporting participant IDs, the exact observed behavior, a proposed revision, and how to check the revision. Fix at least one material issue and ask a participant to repeat the affected task on the revised version. Preserve contrary feedback rather than forcing consensus.

## Completion evidence

The feedback milestone requires actual consenting participants, session notes, a synthesis of observed issues, and a documented revision with a follow-up check. This guide and simulated answers do not satisfy that milestone.
